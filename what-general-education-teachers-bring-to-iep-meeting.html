What General Education Teachers Should Bring to an IEP Meeting

You are a required member of the team, and your job is to report what actually happens in the general classroom. Here is the concrete, dated data to bring — grades and work samples, accommodation tracking, what you have tried and what it did, and real strengths — instead of impressions.

The short answer

A general education teacher should come to an IEP meeting with concrete, dated data, not impressions. Bring current grades and a few representative work samples; a record of the student’s existing accommodations and whether you have been providing them and whether they are working; documentation of the interventions you have tried and their results; dated notes on behavior and engagement; and specific examples of the student’s strengths. Your role on the team is to describe how the student actually performs in the general classroom — so the more your report rests on evidence you can point to, the more useful and credible it is.

As a general education teacher, you are a legally required member of the IEP team, and you often have something no one else in the room has: daily, sustained observation of how the student does in the general-education setting. But that value only lands if you can back it with specifics. “She’s doing okay, I think” helps no one write goals or decide services. “She’s at 68% in the class, her extended-time accommodation is clearly helping on tests but she is not using the graphic organizers, and here are two work samples from this month” changes the meeting. This guide is about walking in with the second kind of information.

What is the general education teacher’s role in an IEP meeting?

Your job is not to write the IEP or to run the meeting — that is usually the case manager’s and the team’s. Your job is to be the expert witness on the general classroom: how the student performs against grade-level expectations, how the current accommodations play out in a real room of thirty, what you have tried, and what the student does well. You contribute directly to the “present levels” picture, you help judge whether accommodations are realistic and effective, and you flag concerns early. Coming prepared is also a professional obligation: accommodations in an IEP are legally required, and part of what the team checks is whether they are actually being delivered — which means your data on fidelity matters.

What should a general education teacher bring to an IEP meeting?

Here is the concrete list. You will not need every item for every meeting, but each earns its place when the topic comes up.

1
Current grades and representative work samples
The student’s standing in your class right now, plus two or three work samples that show what they can do independently and where they struggle. Concrete evidence beats a letter grade alone.
2
Accommodation tracking and fidelity data
Which accommodations the student currently has, how often they are used, and whether they are working. “Extended time is helping; preferential seating made no difference; he declines the reader” is exactly what the team needs to refine the plan.
3
Data on what you have tried
The interventions and strategies you have used and their results. This shows the general classroom has been responsive and gives the team a tested starting point rather than a blank slate.
4
Dated behavior and engagement notes
If behavior or attention is relevant, bring a dated record — frequency, patterns, what precedes it — not a general impression. The same objective, contemporaneous standard that applies to any behavior record applies here.
5
Specific strengths and what motivates the student
Real examples of what the student does well, enjoys, and responds to. Strengths are required input for a good IEP, and you are often the person who sees them most.
6
Attendance, lates, and participation data if relevant
Patterns in attendance or class participation can be quietly central to a student’s struggles, and a dated record surfaces them where memory would not.
7
Your questions and concerns, written down
What you need to serve this student better, and anything you want the team to address. Writing them beforehand means they do not get lost in the flow of the meeting.

Why does accommodation tracking matter so much?

Because an accommodation that exists on paper but is not delivered — or is delivered and does not help — is a problem the team can only fix if someone reports it, and that someone is usually you. IEP accommodations are legally binding: the school is obligated to provide them, and undelivered accommodations are a genuine compliance and equity issue. Just as important, the team needs to know which ones actually work in your room. If you can say, with dated examples, that a support is helping, being ignored, or unrealistic given how your class runs, you turn the accommodations section from a wish list into a plan grounded in reality. Vague reassurance that “we’re doing the accommodations” helps no one; specific fidelity data helps everyone.

What kind of data shows what you’ve tried?

The most persuasive “what I’ve tried” data pairs an action with a dated result: moved to front row on the 6th, no change in work completion through the 20th; began chunking assignments into three parts on the 21st, completion rose from about half to most. You do not need a research study — you need enough of a dated trail that the team can see the classroom has been actively problem-solving and can tell what is worth continuing. This is far easier to produce if you have been keeping a running, dated record all along rather than trying to remember six weeks of adjustments the morning of the meeting. A per-student log of behavior, engagement, attendance, and the strategies you have used — the kind a tool like PrepPeriod keeps, timestamped and printable — makes assembling this straightforward, though a disciplined dated notes doc does the same job.

What are common mistakes general education teachers make?

Avoid these

Showing up with only impressions. “He’s a good kid but struggles” is not data. Bring grades, samples, and dated notes so your input can actually shape goals and services.

Not knowing the student’s current accommodations. Review the existing IEP or the accommodations summary before the meeting. Being unsure what the student is entitled to undermines your credibility and, more importantly, the student.

No fidelity data. Being unable to say whether the accommodations are being used and working is a missed chance to fix a plan that is not serving the student.

Only reporting problems. A report that is all deficits is both unfair and less useful. Strengths are required input and often unlock the most effective strategies.

Using labels or offering a diagnosis. Describe behaviors and performance; do not diagnose or use loaded labels. That is outside your role and can harm the process.

Not writing your concerns down. Meetings move fast. If you do not note your questions beforehand, you will remember them in the parking lot afterward.

How should I contribute during the meeting?

Be specific, be balanced, and stay in your lane as the general-classroom expert. Lead with the student’s strengths — it is honest and it sets a collaborative tone with the family — then report performance and accommodation fidelity with your evidence in hand. Describe behavior and struggles in objective terms and let the specialists interpret and diagnose. Ask your written questions. And frame everything as shared problem-solving: you are not there to defend your class or to hand off a student, but to give the team the clearest possible picture of what happens between the bells so the plan they write actually fits the room the student sits in.

Frequently asked questions

What should a general education teacher bring to an IEP meeting?

Bring current grades and a few representative work samples, a record of the student's current accommodations and whether you have been providing them and whether they help, data on the interventions you have tried and their results, dated behavior and engagement notes if relevant, specific examples of the student's strengths, and your questions written down. The theme is concrete, dated evidence rather than general impressions.

Is the general education teacher required to attend the IEP meeting?

Yes. Under federal special-education law, at least one of the student's general education teachers is a required member of the IEP team when the student is or may be participating in the general education environment. Your role is to describe how the student performs in the general classroom, how accommodations are working, and what you have tried, which directly informs the team's decisions.

Why does tracking accommodations matter for the IEP team?

Because IEP accommodations are legally required, and the team can only refine them if someone reports whether they are actually being delivered and whether they work. As the general education teacher, you are usually that someone. Dated fidelity data lets you say which accommodations are helping, being ignored, or unrealistic in your room, turning the plan into something grounded in what really happens.

What data should I keep during the year for IEP meetings?

Keep a running, dated record of the student's performance, the accommodations you provide and their effect, the strategies you try and their results, and any relevant behavior, engagement, or attendance patterns. Recording these as they happen, rather than reconstructing them before the meeting, makes the data more accurate and far easier to assemble. A per-student log, whether an app or a disciplined notes doc, is ideal.

Should general education teachers talk about student strengths in an IEP meeting?

Absolutely. Strengths are required input for a well-written IEP, and the general education teacher often sees them most clearly. Specific examples of what a student does well, enjoys, and responds to frequently point toward the most effective strategies and goals. A report that is all deficits is both unfair to the student and less useful to the team.
